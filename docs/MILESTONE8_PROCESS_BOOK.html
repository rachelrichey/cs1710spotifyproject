3. Data pipeline

Our raw data is the Kaggle Spotify Tracks dataset (114,000 tracks, 114 genres) that our team extended with a release date for each track (from Wikipedia, MusicBrainz and Deezer) and an artist gender label (mostly from Wikidata).

  1. Clean (pipeline/clean_data.py): release dates come in four formats, and two-digit years are ambiguous ("/69" could be 1969 or 2069). We resolve the century by matching the date to the sources cited in the notes column, and fall back to a pivot only when there is no match (2,827 tracks). Dates outside 1950–2022 are flagged, multi-artist gender strings become explicit flags, and duration is converted to seconds.
  2. Aggregate (pipeline/build_story_data.py): builds 11 small JSON files, one per chart. Our definitions are set once at the top of this file: a hit is popularity ≥ 60, a sad song has valence < 0.40, and time-based charts use 1960–2022.
  3. Load: the website fetches only these JSON files and never the raw CSV.

After cleaning: 89,740 unique tracks, 76,275 with a release year in 1960–2022, and 9,263 hits. Automated tests rerun the headline numbers so the site and this process book cannot drift apart.

Change from Milestone 7: the genre comparison now counts 84 genres with at least 30 songs in both periods (before 2000 and since 2010), and 71 of them (85%) got sadder. Our earlier draft said 84% of 83 genres; the final pipeline's numbers are the ones used throughout the site.

4. Website structure and storytelling

The site follows Brent Dykes's data storytelling arc from our storyboard:

  • Setting (chapters 1–2): two songs fifty years apart hook the reader, and the mood map teaches our two axes, valence (sad to happy) and energy (calm to intense).
  • Rising insights (3–5): hits got sadder by decade; this holds within genres; the happy-and-energetic corner of the mood map empties out over time.
  • Main message (6): the radar shows today's hit is sadder but more danceable and more energetic than a 1960s hit: "a sad song you can dance to."
  • Solution and next steps (7–8): takeaways, then readers search their own songs and see which decade they sound like.

Two chapters sit after the arc: the novel view (9) and documentation of the data (10).

Design: a dark, Spotify-inspired theme. A fixed "now playing" bar at the bottom uses scroll position as the playhead, with the chapter title, progress, previous/next buttons and an auto-scroll play button. In chapters 2, 3, 5 and 6 the chart stays pinned while text cards scroll past, and each card changes what the chart shows. On phones the chart pins to the top and the cards scroll underneath.

6. Novel view: genre bursts (sketch 11)

One glyph per genre, built from 28 sampled tracks of that genre. Because every burst uses the same encoding, the glyphs work as small multiples that can be compared side by side.

Why it is novel: no standard chart shows seven attributes of a genre in one compact, repeatable shape. In V1, techno reads at a glance as a narrow, blue, tightly packed fan, while salsa is wide and gold.

What we will test next: whether readers understand the roots and ripples as data rather than decoration; how to compare more than a handful of the 114 genres at once; and linking the bursts to the mood map and year slider.

8. How we used Claude

We used Claude to write the first version of the website code from our storyboard and process book, to build the data pipeline, and to test the site (automated data checks and screenshots on desktop and phone). Claude's first pass had problems that were then fixed: charts disappearing when pinned, dumbbell rows stacked on top of each other, dots misaligned with the axes, overlapping labels on clustered genres, horizontal scrolling on phones, and one wrong sentence (it said 40% of 1990s hits sounded sad; the data says 37%).
